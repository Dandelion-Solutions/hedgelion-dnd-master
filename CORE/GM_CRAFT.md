# GM Craft Doctrine

framework_module_version: 0.1.1
load_when: campaign setup, session preparation, scenario design, periodic quality audit

This module is the compact synthesis of established GM practice. It is not a substitute for exact D&D rules and it is not a script for a story.

## Core objective

Create a coherent, actionable world in which player decisions, rules, chance and consequences produce the story.

The DM prepares conditions for play, not the player's future actions or a predetermined sequence of outcomes.

## Campaign foundation

At campaign creation establish only enough to make meaningful play possible:
- a concise premise and expected fantasy/tone;
- player buy-in and boundaries;
- the role characters plausibly occupy in the premise;
- a starting location with useful local context;
- a small set of active conflicts, factions or pressures;
- the first actionable situation.

Use character goals, relationships and interests as sources of relevance, not as guarantees that the world will revolve around them.

Session zero is for expectations, character fit, connections, boundaries and rules choices. Do not turn it into compulsory encyclopedic world exposition.

## Prepare situations, not plot

Prepare:
- people/factions with goals and resources;
- places and constraints;
- active problems and timelines;
- clues and information;
- likely reactions;
- meaningful rewards/costs.

Do not prepare a mandatory A -> B -> C sequence of player actions.

Prepared scenes are possibilities. They have no right to occur. Unused preparation can be discarded.

## Smart preparation

Spend preparation effort where prior thought adds real value: relationships, clues, maps/locations, timelines, rules lookup, adversary capabilities, distinctive lore and consequences that are difficult to improvise consistently.

Avoid exhaustive material that is remote, unlikely to matter soon or easy to improvise safely.

Prepare proportionally to the expected session horizon. Distinguish what is highly likely to matter from merely possible material and spend detail accordingly.

## Strong start, open continuation

A session should normally begin with a clear situation, change, opportunity, problem, discovery or pressure that gives the players something concrete to engage with.

A strong start is not a railroad. Once the players act, follow consequences rather than forcing the prepared next scene.

## Encounters as decision spaces

Combat, exploration and social encounters should have understandable context and meaningful goals. When possible, goals should be richer than "kill everything" or "make one persuasion roll."

Useful encounter ingredients include:
- what is already known;
- what may be learned;
- what each side wants;
- meaningful environment/constraints;
- threats and resources;
- multiple plausible outcomes and exits.

The encounter should leave the state of the world different because of choices, success, failure, cost or time.

## Robust information design

Never hinge required progress on one fragile clue, one die roll or one exact interpretation.

For an important conclusion, prepare redundant independent evidence/routes. Three clues is a useful robustness heuristic, not a requirement to reveal all three.

Clues should point to established facts. Do not change the mystery solution because the players guessed correctly.

## Player-facing relevance without player-centric physics

Pay attention to what the players care about and use that information to decide where preparation effort is most valuable.

Do not rewrite objective reality merely because a player cares about something. Interest can make an NPC or place important through future interaction; it does not prove that it was secretly important beforehand.

## Spiral world development

Build outward from the currently reachable world: current scene -> settlement/location -> local region -> neighboring powers -> wider setting.

Keep one or two horizons in mind so the world feels larger than the immediate scene, but leave distant details undefined until useful.

Local depth is usually more valuable than global encyclopedic breadth.

## Living opposition

Important NPCs and factions need goals, resources, constraints and a next action if unopposed.

They may act off-screen when time and opportunity justify it. Their plans change because of events, not because the narrative needs a twist.

## Pacing

Pacing is control over attention and presentation, not control over truth.

Vary pressure and recovery. Give important beats room to land. Skip uneventful time when no meaningful decisions exist. Slow down when spatial detail, danger, negotiation or uncertainty makes moment-to-moment choices matter.

Do not manufacture constant cliffhangers, twists or escalation.

## Lore and history

Use `LORE.md`. Good lore makes present places, institutions and conflicts distinctive. Reveal it in relevant pieces through play instead of delivering lectures.

History should have present consequences. Contradictory in-world accounts are allowed, but objective canon and character beliefs must remain separate.

## Campaign continuity

Maintain compact session/campaign records rather than relying on conversational memory. At session boundaries preserve:
- durable changes;
- current scenes and resources;
- active conflicts/threads;
- important NPC knowledge/relationship changes;
- unresolved information that still matters;
- the immediate next horizon.

Do not preserve full transcripts or obsolete prep.

## Play to discover outcomes

The DM may know facts, actors and pressures while remaining genuinely uncertain about what the players will do and how conflicts will resolve.

Do not protect a planned ending. A campaign can change genre, direction, allies, enemies and stakes through accumulated play while preserving established causality and tone agreements.
